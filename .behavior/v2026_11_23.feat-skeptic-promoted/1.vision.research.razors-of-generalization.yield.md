# research: razors of generalization and abstraction

> the human asked: "are there any razors of generalization and abstraction? e.g., separate the forest from the trees?"
> answer: yes. no single classic razor bears the name, but nine questions hold up at a source. they form one group, `shaves=scope`.
> method: websearch and webfetch (no bhrowser; `rhx list` was blocked by a hook in this run). each source was read as text; a quote is verbatim as the fetch returned it. `guess:` marks a claim not read at a source.
> the gerund hook blocks some verbatim quotes and some titles. where a quote or title had a gerund, it is cut or paraphrased and marked "(cut: gerund)".
> test for add: does it ask a question no catalog razor already asks?

## .the frame

a razor is a question put to a claim. mode is one of observation (a read or a count), logic (inspect the claim's form), rivals (named alternatives weighed).

a scope claim has a level. the level is the set of cases the claim covers. each razor below asks if the claim sits at the level its proof sits at.

## .candidates, verified

### hasty generalization (alias: secundum quid, law of small numbers)

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | classical informal fallacy. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasty_generalization: "an informal fallacy wherein a conclusion is drawn about all or many instances of a phenomenon on the basis of one or a few instances of that phenomenon." form read there: "The proportion Q of the sample has attribute A. Therefore, the proportion Q of the population has attribute A." the page lists "Secundum quid" among the alternate names. the Aristotle root of secundum quid: `guess:` not read. |
| question | how many cases back this "all", and how were they drawn? |
| settled by | observation (count the cases; read how they were picked) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | one case suffices where the claim is existential ("some"), or where logic gives the law. one counter-case refutes an "all" (popper). a rule of small n can be waved at every claim until none stands. a large count is no cure if the draw is biased (see survivorship). |
| verdict | add. base rate asks how often the class holds. survivorship asks what the filter dropped. neither asks if the claim's scope word ("all", "always", "any") outruns the count behind it. |

### fallacy of composition and of division

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | classical. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_composition: "The fallacy of composition is an informal fallacy that arises when one infers that something is true of the whole" (the text ends there in the fetch) and "The fallacy of composition is the converse of the fallacy of division." the Aristotle attribution (Sophistical Refutations): `guess:` the page gave none. |
| question | is a trait of the parts stated of the whole, or a trait of the whole stated of a part? does that trait transfer? |
| settled by | logic (name the trait; test if it is additive or emergent) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | some traits do transfer (mass adds; "every part is steel" gives "the whole is steel"). a ban on all part-to-whole moves would cut sound sums. the test is per trait, never per form. |
| verdict | add, as one row with two directions. no catalog razor asks about the move between part and whole. this is the plain form of the human's "forest from trees". |

### ecological fallacy

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | W. S. Robinson, "Ecological Correlations and the Behavior of Individuals," American Sociological Review 15(3): 351-357, 1950. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_fallacy: "a formal fallacy in the interpretation of statistical data" in which "inferences about the nature of individuals are deduced from inferences about the group to which those individuals belong." Robinson, 1930 census by state: state-level correlation -0.53 between illiteracy and the foreign-born share; individual-level +0.12. the page adds that a 2011 correction put the state-level value at -0.46. who coined the term: `guess:` not read. |
| question | at what unit was the data read, and is the claim stated at that unit? |
| settled by | observation (read the unit of the data) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | group data can bound the individual case (ecological inference methods): `guess:` not read. |
| verdict | reject as a separate row. it is division (above) in a statistical dress. cite it as the worked case under division. |

### simpson's paradox

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Simpson 1951; named by Blyth 1972; earlier notes by Pearson and Yule. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson%27s_paradox: "a trend appears in several groups of data" but "disappears or reverses when the groups are combined." the page says Simpson "first described this phenomenon in a technical paper in 1951" and "The name Simpson's paradox was introduced by Colin R. Blyth in 1972." example read: fall 1973 UC Berkeley graduate admission; the pooled gap favored men; the department-level data showed "a small but statistically significant bias in favor of women." |
| question | does this trend hold inside each subgroup, or only in the pool? |
| settled by | observation (split the data by the likeliest hidden group; compare) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | the split can be wrong: a split by a variable the cause runs through can hide the true effect. which level is the true one is a causal claim, not a data read: `guess:` the page text read did not state this. a hunt for splits will also find a reversal by chance. |
| verdict | add. the only razor that tells the seat what read to run to test a part-whole claim. composition and division are the logic; this is the count. |

### fallacy of misplaced concreteness

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Whitehead, Science and the Modern World, 1925, pp. 52 and 58. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_misplaced_concreteness: p. 52 names "the accidental error of" taking the abstract for the concrete (cut: gerund); p. 58: "the real error is an example of what I have termed: The Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness." the page calls it a reification: an abstraction "treated as if it were a concrete real event or physical entity." |
| question | is the noun this claim names a real item, or a label for a class, a score, or a model? |
| settled by | logic (inspect the noun: can one point at it, or only at its instances?) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | some abstractions are the best available handle and carry real force (a "bug", a "team"). the razor shaves the claim that gives the label a cause or a will. counter: box (the model is still useful). |
| verdict | add. a model stated as the world. no catalog razor asks if a noun has a referent. |

### "seek simplicity and distrust it"

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Whitehead, The Concept of Nature, 1920 (Tarner lectures, Nov 1919), ch. 7. read in a search result only, from Oxford Reference: https://oxfordreference.com/view/10.1093/acref/9780191826719.001.0001/q-oro-ed4-00011471. read: "We are apt to fall into the error of thinking that the facts are simple because simplicity is the goal of our quest." and the motto "Seek simplicity and distrust it." (the words between them hold a gerund; cut.) page number differs by source (163 vs 143). the book itself: not read. |

| settled by | rivals (a richer rival read on the same facts) |

| verdict | reject as a row. anti-razor already asks it and cites Einstein 1933. cite Whitehead as a second source under anti-razor. |

### "the map is not the territory"

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Korzybski, 1931 paper "A Non-Aristotelian System and Its Necessity for Rigour in Mathematics and Physics" (AAAS, New Orleans); reprinted in Science and Sanity, 1933. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map%E2%80%93territory_relation: "a map is not the territory"; the page glosses it as: an abstraction is not the item it stands for, and an accurate map has a "similar structure" to the territory. the original text of the paper: `guess:` not read. |
| question | does this claim give the map's trait to the land? |
| settled by | logic |
| failure mode / counter-razor | same as whitehead: an accurate map is useful. |
| verdict | reject as a row. the same blade as whitehead, with a later date and a wider fame. keep as the plain-words name in the brief. |

### ladder of abstraction

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | S. I. Hayakawa, Language in Action, 1939 (later Language in Thought and Action; the common diagram is from the 1949 update). the primary text was not read. the en.wikipedia page for it returned 404. read only in a websearch summary of secondary pages (Big Think, MindTools, a Lumen course page): the bessie rungs run "process cow", the cow we perceive, "Bessie", "cow", "livestock", "asset", "wealth"; each step up "loses more of the original concrete experience"; Hayakawa also warned of "dead-level" abstraction (cut: gerund). sources differ on the rungs. all of it is secondary: `guess:` for every detail. |
| question | on which rung does this claim sit, and on which rung does its evidence sit? name one concrete case the claim covers. |
| settled by | observation (name the case; find it in the record) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | no fixed count of rungs. a claim pitched at one level all through can be at fault in either direction: all general, or all anecdote. a case named from memory may be made up. |
| verdict | add, flagged as secondary-sourced. it is the one razor that runs in both directions: down (name the case) and up (name the class). it carries the human's forest and trees. confirm at the 1939 text before it goes in a brief. |

### law of leaky abstractions

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Joel Spolsky, 11 Nov 2002, https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/. read: "All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky." |
| question | what does this layer hide, and could the hidden part break the claim made from above it? |
| settled by | observation (read below the layer: the log, the source, the failure) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | a leak is a matter of degree ("to some degree"); the law gives no count of leaks. an urge to look under every layer is a cost, and most layers hold. |
| verdict | add. a claim made from the top of a stack ("the api returns x, so x") asserts that the abstraction is sealed. chesterton asks why a fence stands; none asks what is under the cover. the law is a software law, not a general one: `guess:` its use for claims outside code is the role's gloss. |

### rule of three, and the wrong abstraction

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Don Roberts, popularized by Fowler, 1999 (the book on code cleanup; title cut: gerund). read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(computer_programming): "when similar code is used three times, it should be extracted into a new procedure"; the page adds that early cleanup can pick a wrong abstraction. Sandi Metz, RailsConf 2014 talk (title cut: gerund) and the post https://sandimetz.com/blog/2016/1/20/the-wrong-abstraction. read: "duplication is far cheaper than the wrong abstraction" and "prefer duplication over the wrong abstraction." the Fowler text itself: `guess:` not read. |
| question | have enough real cases shown the shape to name it? |
| settled by | observation (count the cases) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | three is a convention, not a proof. the source of the number three, beyond habit: `guess:`. the opposite error also exists: a copy that drifts in many places (the page reads this as the cost of a replicated rule). |
| verdict | reject as a row. it is hasty generalization put in a unit of action, with a number set by habit. the Metz line is the best statement of the cost of an early law and fits the group note below. |

### "cannot see the forest for the trees"

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | More, Confutacion of Tyndals Answere, 1533: "And then we may well tell hym agayne, that he can not se the wood for the trees." | question | none put to a claim. the idiom names a fault (a view too close) and omits its twin (a view too far: all forest, no trees). |

| failure mode / counter-razor | an idiom for one direction only. hayakawa covers both. |

### "all models are wrong"

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | George Box. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_models_are_wrong: the 1987 book by Box and Draper on response surfaces, p. 424 (title cut: gerund): "Essentially, all models are wrong, but some are useful." the page ties the phrase to a 1976 JASA paper, "Science and Statistics", but gave no verbatim 1976 words; so the exact 1976 words: `guess:` not read. |
| question | for what use is this model held, and is its error small enough for that use? |
| settled by | rivals (the model against a simpler and a richer rival, for the same use) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | "all models are wrong" is a shield for a sloppy model. the half "some are useful" needs a test of use; that test is the razor. |
| verdict | add. the question is purpose-relative adequacy. anti-razor asks for fit to all reads; box asks for fit to the one use. whitehead asks if the model is taken as the world; box asks if the model fits its job. |

### overfit

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | statistics, no single author. read at the Wikipedia page on overfit (the url ends in the gerund form; cut). read: the term names "the production of an analysis that corresponds too closely or exactly to a particular set of data" and may fail on new data. the page lists techniques that test a model on data kept out of the fit, and: "The optimal function usually needs verification on bigger or completely new datasets." |
| question | was this claim fitted to the reads it now cites, and did it meet any read it was not fitted to? |
| settled by | observation (hold-out reads: a set kept apart before the fit) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | no fresh reads may exist. a hold-out reused many times leaks (it becomes a train set): `guess:` not read at the page. overlap: popper part 2 (was the refuter run) and feynman (a test whose result was set before the look). |
| verdict | add, borderline. the new part is order: reads that shaped the claim cannot also test it. a seat that sees a lesson derived from one run and "confirmed" by the same run needs this. |

### external validity

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Cook and Campbell, 1979 book on quasi-experiment design (title cut: gerund); the page credits them for the split between a move "to" a population and a move "across" subpopulations. read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_validity: "External validity is the validity of" use of the conclusions of a study "outside the context of that study." (verb cut: gerund). the page did not name Campbell and Stanley 1963; that earlier date: `guess:`. |
| question | does the place the claim was shown match the place it is now used? |
| settled by | observation (compare the two places on the traits that could change the result) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | no two places match in all traits; the razor needs a named trait that matters. a mismatch in a trait that does not matter is no fault. |
| verdict | add. base rate already carries the reference-class problem (the class pick); that row asks how often a class holds. this asks if the proof case is one of the class. a lesson from repo A stated of repo B is the common case for the seat. |

### hume's problem of induction

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | Hume, Treatise 1739-40 and Enquiry 1748. read at https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/induction-problem/: the problem asks "on what grounds we come to our beliefs about the unobserved on the basis of inductive inferences." the page names the Uniformity Principle, which in the Treatise reads: "that instances, of which we have had no experience, must resemble those, of which we have had experience." ### "the exception proves the rule"

| field | finding |
|---|---|
| author, year, source | read at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exception_that_proves_the_rule: the adage has a contested sense (the page's own words hold a gerund; cut). the Latin, thought to be the root: exceptio probat regulam in casibus non exceptis, "the exception proves the rule in cases not excepted"; Cicero, defence of Balbus, is credited. the second half is "almost always" dropped in modern use. readings: the exception implies a rule exists elsewhere; or "prove" meant "test"; or a rare exception shows the rule is strong. the page marks the last as "literally incorrect." |
| question | does this counter-case bound the rule, or is it used to guard the rule from the case? |
| settled by | logic (does the rule name its exception ahead of the case, as the Latin tail "in cases not excepted" implies?) |
| failure mode / counter-razor | used as a shield, it makes a rule that no case can refute. that is popper's fault and duhem-quine's abuse. |
| verdict | reject as a razor. keep as a hazard note: an exception named after the case is a patch, not a proof. an exception named before it, with the cases it leaves, is a scope bound. |

## .the group `shaves=scope`

the human's gloss: "a claim at the wrong level: a part stated as the whole, a whole stated of a part, a case stated as a law, a model stated as the world."

| shape | razors (all adds) | mode |
|---|---|---|
| a part stated as the whole | composition (the part-to-whole half), simpson's paradox | logic, observation |
| a whole stated of a part | division (the whole-to-part half), ecological fallacy as its worked case | logic |
| a case stated as a law | hasty generalization, external validity, overfit | observation |
| a model stated as the world | whitehead's misplaced concreteness, leaky abstractions, box | logic, observation, rivals |
| the level itself | hayakawa's ladder: name the rung of the claim and the rung of the proof | observation |

- nine adds: hasty generalization, composition and division (one row), simpson, whitehead, hayakawa, leaky abstractions, box, overfit, external validity.
- rejects: ecological fallacy (folded under division), "seek simplicity" (folded under anti-razor), korzybski (folded under whitehead), rule of three and metz (folded under hasty generalization), forest and trees idiom (the plain name of the group), reference class (folded under base rate), hume's induction (the floor), exception proves the rule (a hazard note).
- the group is sorted by shape, as the prior research proposes: the seat finds a razor by what the claim looks like. each of these claims has a scope word or a noun to catch: "all", "always", "the system", "the model", "the api".
- order of use: `guess:` first name the rung (hayakawa), then pick the shape.

### does foundation vs application belong in it?

- yes, with a change of role. the role's razor says: split each lesson into the law that holds in any domain and the act it owes here. that is a scope question: which part of this lesson is a law, and which is a case?
- the group's razors shave a claim that is at the wrong level. foundation vs application does not shave; it sorts a lesson into two correct levels. it is the group's repair move.
- its test, from the same text: strip the domain nouns from the lesson; does it still hold? that is logic. then name a second domain where it holds; that is observation (hayakawa's "name a case", and external validity). the act is what remains when the domain nouns are put back.
- risks, each with its razor from the group: a "law" drawn from one case (hasty generalization); a "law" from a place unlike the next (external validity); a "law" so high up that it cannot fail (hayakawa's top rung, "wealth" in the bessie example); a law fitted to the run that gave it (overfit).
- the counter-razor from Metz: a law named too early is costly. the foundation half should wait for a second case, or carry a mark that it is a first case.
- the origin of foundation vs application as a razor is the role's own; no outside source read. whether it matches Hayakawa up and down the ladder: `guess:`.
