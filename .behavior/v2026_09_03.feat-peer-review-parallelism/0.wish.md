# wish: peer reviews at one level run concurrently — and each level declares its own concurrency

## .what

two outcomes, and the second is what makes the first shippable:

1. **peer reviewers at the same level run CONCURRENTLY.** today they run one after another, so a
   level of four reviewers costs the sum of four durations when it could cost the longest one.
2. **each level declares its own concurrency.** `l1` may fan out without bound; `l3` may permit
   exactly one at a time, because the brain behind it is rate limited.

the level GATE is untouched. a level still does not open until the level before it is terminal.
what changes is what happens *within* a level.

## .the wisher's own words

> *"we want to finally do that peer review paralellism. except we want it to have a bottleneck
> ability. e.g., each level can specify the concurrency. (l1 is infinite, l3 is 1 at a time due to
> ratelimits)"*

recorded verbatim.

## .why

### the serial cost is the whole cost

a level of four reviewers at ~35s, ~52s, ~28s, ~20s costs **~135s** today. run together it costs
**~52s** — the longest one. that ratio grows with the reviewer count, and the reviewer count grows
every time a rubric is added.

a driver pays this on **every** `--as arrived`, on **every** iteration of a convergence loop. so
the cost is not one wait; it is one wait per round, times every round, times every stone.

### the cap is not a follow-up — it is what makes fan-out safe

full fan-out at a rate limited level does not merely run slow. it **fails**: the calls 429, the
lane returns a malfunction, and the driver diagnoses a reviewer that was never broken.

so *"run them all at once"* is not shippable on its own. the two outcomes above ship together or
not at all, and that is why this wish carries both rather than one.

### the level gate already holds the correctness

`isReviewPeerLevelTerminal` decides when a level is done. that predicate is what guarantees l1's
verdicts inform l3's run, and it is entirely orthogonal to how many reviewers execute at once
within l1. so concurrency here does not weaken the ladder — it leaves the ladder's own guarantee
exactly where it sits.

## .acceptance

1. **reviewers at one level execute concurrently.** wall clock for a level approaches its slowest
   member rather than the sum of its members
2. **the level gate survives.** a level opens only once the level before it is terminal, on the
   same predicate as today. no verdict crosses a level boundary early
3. **each level declares a concurrency.** the declaration is per level, and a level with no
   declaration behaves sensibly (state which default you chose, and why)
4. **`l3 = 1` genuinely serializes.** exactly one review is in flight at that level, provable —
   not merely likely under a scheduler that happens to be slow
5. **`l1` unbounded genuinely fans out.** the count in flight is the count declared at that level
6. **the shared meter does not race.** two reviews that settle at once must not lose one another's
   write. state what you did about it
7. **a clamp with teeth.** a test that goes red under the serial arm and green under the concurrent
   one, and a test that goes red where a `1` cap admits two in flight. prove they bite — revert,
   see red, restore, see green

## .the boundary — what is NOT in scope

- **do not change what a verdict MEANS.** approved / rejected / exhausted / malfunction keep their
  present semantics
- **do not change the level ladder.** levels remain ordered and gated; only intra-level execution
  changes
- **do not touch self reviews.** they are a different ladder with different rules
- **do not lower the bar to make the clock look better.** a review that is skipped is not a review
  that is fast

## ⚠️ .the live-stream renderer is DESIRED, never required

a concurrent run wants a display that shows several in-flight lines at once, and the extant
formatter renders one lane at a time.

**this is the piece most likely to eat the whole round.** so it is subordinate: if you must pick,
deliver acceptance 1–7 with a plain sequential render of results as they settle, and say so. a
correct concurrent run with a dull display is a success. a beautiful display over a serial run is
not.

### four hazards it flagged

1. **the meter-write race** — two reviews that settle together can clobber one another's write.
   acceptance 6 exists for this
2. **resource pressure under full fan-out** — several brains at once cost memory and quota, which
   is precisely what the per-level cap answers
3. **completion order ≠ declared order** — a concurrent run settles out of order, so any consumer
   that reads results positionally breaks. worth a look at the formatter
4. ⚠️ **a stale doc** — `RouteStoneGuard.ts:53` claims *"higher levels run first"* while the code
   runs LOWER first (`:307`). one of the two is wrong. this is a **demonstration** of a defect you
   will meet, never an instruction to fix it — though it is cheap to correct on contact

### one caution on a WORD it raised

it floated `mutex` for the concurrency-of-one case and then argued against it: `mutex` reads as a
cross-level lock, and this is a per-level bound. that argument looks sound and it is **a proposal**
— the word is yours to settle in-repo, per this repo's own term rules.
