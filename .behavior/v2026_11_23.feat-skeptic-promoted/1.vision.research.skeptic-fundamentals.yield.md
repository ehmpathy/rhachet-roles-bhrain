## .3 the Peirce verdict

hypothesis under test: the three answers (a read, logic alone, rivals weighed) map onto Peirce's induction, deduction, abduction.

### what Peirce wrote

- three kinds, found by the swap of terms in the syllogism Barbara. S1: deduction is "necessary inference"; induction is "argument from random sample to population" and "is not necessary inference"; abduction is "a form of probable argument entirely different from both deduction and induction. It has the air of conjecture or 'educated guess' about it."
- in the mature view they are **phases of one method, not three kinds of answer**. S1: "Scientific method begins with abduction or hypothesis"; abduction conjectures, deduction derives what the hypothesis implies, induction tests it; in his most mature philosophy he "virtually (perhaps totally and literally) equates the trichotomy with the three phases he discerns in the scientific method".
- each carries a **different measure of doubt**. S1: "Probability he associated with deduction. Verisimilitude he associated with induction. And plausibility he associated with abduction." The three are "mutually incommensurable".

### the map

| the human's answer | Peirce's mode | fit | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| logic alone | deduction | **holds** | a necessary inference; the conclusion cannot fail if the premises hold. in the loop it also derives what a hypothesis predicts |
| a read, an observation | induction | **holds in part** | for Peirce induction is the test phase and, in the basic case, the step from a sample to a population (S1). a lone observation is data; it becomes a verdict through induction. S7: the step from cases to the general rests on no demonstrative or probable argument (Hume) |
| rivals weighed | abduction | **holds in part; it mixes two eras** | Peirce's abduction is the **making of the conjecture** ("educated guess"). the **weigh** is inference to the best explanation, which S2 says Peirce's own statement "does not capture what most nowadays understand by abduction". the weigh also runs through deduction and induction in Peirce's loop: deduce each rival's prediction, observe, drop the rival that fails (the ACH step in S14) |

### verdict: confirmed as to warrant, refined as to use, refuted as a partition

- **confirmed:** the three answers differ in the *warrant* they carry, and Peirce's modes are the standard names for the three. logic gives necessity. a read gives verisimilitude, how far a sample backs a general claim. rivals give plausibility (S1).
- **refuted:** that a question is answered by *one* of the three. in Peirce they are phases of one loop: abduction names the rival, deduction says what each rival forbids, induction runs the read that tells them apart. a full answer to "what would show this wrong?" uses all three in order, so "a read" is the last phase, never the whole answer. S3 agrees from the other side: a read tests a claim only with its auxiliaries, so a read alone cannot say which claim it refuted.
- **refined:** a question *leaves one answer* when a step of the loop forces it. logic forces it when the rival is inconsistent with what is held (deduction; the elenchus, S13). a read forces it when the rivals predict different observations and the read is clean; S3 says that case is rare, since Duhem denies a "crucial experiment" that "definitively" confirms one rival and refutes the other. a question *leaves rivals* when the read fits several (S14, S2).
- **new point, not in the hypothesis:** the answer by rivals should carry the label *plausible*, not *probable* (S1). a reply that says "the best explanation is X" claims plausibility; it owes no probability and should state none.
- **new point:** abduction needs the rival named. S2 ("best of a bad lot", after van Fraassen): "hardly ever will we have considered, or will it even be possible to consider, all potential explanations", and the catch-all "none of the above" rival is "hardly informative". the weigh is only as good as the list, so the act to list rivals is itself a question to ask.
- **a read can imply one solution that must be questioned:** the human's own point is held by S3 (W3 below): the read tests the claim and its auxiliaries as one bundle.

## .4 the overfit-to-reads cautions

where the canon cautions that the read, the observation, or the search for evidence is not the whole of the method.

| # | the caution | source | passage read |
|---|---|---|---|
| W1 | **a read has a theory in it.** how much an observation proves depends on the theoretical claims behind the instrument and the path | S4 | "The evidential value of an observation has been assumed to depend on how sensitive it is to whatever it is used to study. But this in turn depends on the adequacy of any theoretical claims its sensitivity may depend on" (the thermometer example) |
| W2 | **it is not always a harm.** the theory in the read can be benign and even needed | S4 | the theory to be tested is "often benign and even necessary"; a number in a lab notebook does a scientist "little epistemic good" unless she can recruit the background claims to read it |
| W3 | **a read tests a bundle, never one claim.** a hypothesis yields predictions only joined with auxiliaries; there is no crucial experiment | S3 | hypotheses are tested "in groups or collections, never in isolation"; "no experiment ever tells us precisely which belief it is that we must revise or give up" |
| W4 | **the rescue that works and the rescue that fails look alike.** a failed prediction can be met by a new auxiliary (the planet beyond Uranus) or an ad hoc patch (Marxism) | S3, S6 | S3: Newton's theory was "protected from refutation" by a challenge to the seven-planet assumption, which "bore fruit"; S6: Marxism "saved from falsification by the addition of ad hoc hypotheses". the same move; only a later test tells them apart |
